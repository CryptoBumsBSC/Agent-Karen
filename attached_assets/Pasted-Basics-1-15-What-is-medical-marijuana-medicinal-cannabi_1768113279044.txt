Basics (1–15)

What is medical marijuana (medicinal cannabis)?
Medical marijuana is cannabis prescribed/authorised by a clinician to help manage symptoms of a medical condition, using regulated products with known THC/CBD content (rules vary by country/state).

What’s the difference between THC and CBD?
THC is the main intoxicating cannabinoid (“high”) and can impair cognition/coordination; CBD is non-intoxicating but can still cause side effects and drug interactions.

Does CBD get you high?
CBD is generally non-intoxicating (doesn’t produce the typical “high” like THC), but it can cause drowsiness and other side effects in some people.

Is medical cannabis the same as recreational cannabis?
Not necessarily—medical products are typically regulated and prescribed/authorised for symptom management; recreational products vary widely by jurisdiction and may not meet medical-grade standards.

How does cannabis work in the body?
Cannabinoids interact with the endocannabinoid system (CB1/CB2 receptors and related pathways), influencing pain, appetite, mood, nausea, sleep, and more—effects depend on product, dose, and person.

How fast does medical cannabis work?
Inhaled forms act faster; oral forms take longer but last longer. Exact timing varies by product and individual.

How long do effects last?
Oral THC products can have effects that last for hours and may affect next-day functioning in some people; duration varies by route and dose.

Can you overdose on cannabis?
Fatal overdose is rare, but overconsumption—especially of edibles—can cause severe distress (anxiety, panic, vomiting, confusion), and can be dangerous when combined with driving or other risks.

Is medical cannabis “natural,” and does that mean it’s safe?
“Natural” doesn’t equal safe—cannabis can cause side effects, impairment, dependence risk, and drug interactions.

What’s in the cannabis plant besides THC/CBD?
Cannabis contains many cannabinoids and other compounds (including terpenes), and product effects can vary based on composition.

What are terpenes and do they matter medically?
Terpenes contribute to aroma/flavour; claims about specific medical effects are common but clinical evidence for many terpene-specific benefits is still limited.

What does “indica vs sativa” mean medically?
These labels are widely used, but effects are not reliably predicted by “indica/sativa” alone; cannabinoid dose and formulation matter more.

What is the “entourage effect”?
It’s the idea that multiple cannabis compounds work together; it’s plausible but not fully proven in high-quality human trials for most conditions.

Is medical cannabis FDA-approved?
In the U.S., only certain cannabinoid medicines are FDA-approved for specific uses; most dispensary cannabis products are not FDA-approved as drugs.

Is medicinal cannabis regulated in Australia?
Yes—medicinal cannabis is regulated and accessed via clinician prescribing/authorisation pathways (e.g., TGA frameworks), not as ordinary over-the-counter supplements.

Evidence and what it helps (16–35)

What conditions have the strongest evidence for cannabinoids?
High-level reviews report evidence for chemotherapy-induced nausea/vomiting, chronic pain in adults (modest benefit), and MS-related spasticity symptoms (often short-term, certain products).

Does medical cannabis help chronic pain?
Some evidence suggests modest benefit for certain chronic pain, but results vary and side effects can limit use; clinical guidance exists (e.g., TGA guidance for chronic non-cancer pain in Australia).

Does it help nerve pain (neuropathic pain)?
Some studies show benefit in certain neuropathic pain populations, but it’s not universally effective and must be balanced against impairment and adverse effects.

Does cannabis help cancer itself?
Cannabis is used for symptom relief (e.g., nausea, appetite, pain) but is not established as a cancer cure in clinical evidence.

Does it help chemotherapy nausea/vomiting?
Yes—reviews report conclusive evidence that certain oral cannabinoids can help chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting.

Does it help multiple sclerosis (MS) spasticity?
Evidence supports improvement in patient-reported MS spasticity symptoms with certain cannabinoid medicines/products in the short term.

Does it help epilepsy?
A purified CBD medicine is approved for certain rare seizure disorders (e.g., referenced by CDC/FDA); this does not mean all CBD products treat epilepsy.

Does it help anxiety?
Evidence is mixed; THC can worsen anxiety or trigger panic in some people, and benefits are not consistent across trials.

Does it help depression?
Not established as a depression treatment; heavy or high-THC use can be associated with mental health harms in some individuals.

Does it help sleep?
Some people report sleep benefits (often secondary to pain relief), but evidence isn’t strong for many sleep disorders and THC can impair next-day alertness.

Does it help PTSD?
Research is ongoing; some report symptom relief, but evidence quality varies and THC may worsen anxiety/paranoia in some.

Does it help migraine?
Evidence is still emerging; it’s not a first-line migraine therapy in most clinical guidelines.

Does it help arthritis?
Some people use it for pain, but robust evidence for arthritis-specific outcomes is limited; benefits may reflect general pain relief rather than disease modification.

Does it help inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)?
Some symptom improvement has been reported, but evidence is not definitive and it’s not considered a proven disease-control therapy.

Does it help fibromyalgia?
Evidence is limited/mixed; some people report benefit, but trials vary and adverse effects can be significant.

Does it help endometriosis pain?
Not well established in high-quality trials; symptom relief is reported anecdotally, but evidence remains limited.

Does it help nausea unrelated to chemo?
Cannabinoids can help nausea in some contexts, but they can also cause side effects and in some users heavy use can contribute to cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (severe vomiting).

Does it help appetite/weight loss?
Certain cannabinoid medicines have been used for appetite stimulation (e.g., HIV/AIDS-related cachexia), but benefits and risks depend on product and patient factors.

Is cannabis helpful for glaucoma?
High-level reviews note cannabis is not helpful for glaucoma as a practical medical treatment.

What’s the bottom line on “medical cannabis treats everything”?
That’s misinformation—evidence is condition-specific and often modest, and safety/interaction risks are real.

Access, scripts, and legality (36–50)

How do patients access medicinal cannabis in Australia?
Typically through a doctor/authorised prescriber pathway under TGA frameworks; products and indications are regulated.

Do you need a “medical marijuana card”?
Depends on jurisdiction (common in parts of the U.S., not the same system everywhere). In Australia it’s clinician prescribing/authorisation, not a card-based retail model.

Can any doctor prescribe medicinal cannabis in Australia?
Access is regulated; many doctors can apply/participate via the relevant pathways, but rules and processes apply.

Is medicinal cannabis a first-line treatment?
Usually not—many guidance documents position it after standard therapies, depending on condition and patient risk.

Can pharmacists substitute brands/products?
Rules vary; because cannabinoid ratios and formulations differ, substitution may not be straightforward without prescriber guidance.

Can you travel with medicinal cannabis?
It depends on local laws, destination rules, and documentation; some places treat THC products as illegal regardless of prescription.

Can you take it on a plane?
Air travel rules vary by country and airline; even with a prescription, crossing borders can be legally risky for THC-containing products.

Can you order medicinal cannabis online?
Some jurisdictions allow delivery through regulated pharmacies/clinics; “online CBD” products can be unregulated and mislabeled in some markets.

Is it legal to grow your own for medical use?
Depends entirely on jurisdiction; many places restrict home cultivation even for medical patients.

Can employers ban medicinal cannabis use?
Workplace policies and safety-sensitive roles vary; THC impairment and testing can create legal/work consequences even with prescription.

Will a prescription protect you from roadside drug testing? (Australia)
Not automatically—THC can be detected and drug-driving offences apply; talk with your clinician about legal/road safety implications.

Is CBD legal everywhere?
No—CBD legality varies and many “CBD products” are not regulated as medicines; rules differ by country/state.

Can you advertise medicinal cannabis in Australia?
Advertising is restricted and regulated; regulators have taken enforcement action around unlawful advertising.

Do laws change often?
Yes—medical cannabis regulation evolves (e.g., scheduling, driving laws, research access). Always check current local rules.

Is U.S. federal law changing around marijuana scheduling?
There has been ongoing policy movement and debate; any “rescheduling” affects research and regulation but does not automatically make all products federally legal.

Products and routes (51–65)

What forms does medicinal cannabis come in?
Common forms include oils, capsules, sprays, and sometimes flower for vaporisation (depending on jurisdiction and indication).

What’s the difference between oil, capsules, and flower?
They differ mainly in onset time, duration, dose precision, and lung exposure risk.

Is smoking medicinal cannabis recommended?
Many health authorities prefer non-smoked routes due to respiratory exposure; medical use often focuses on oral/oromucosal preparations.

Is vaping safer than smoking?
It may reduce combustion toxins versus smoking, but inhalation still has risks; product quality and additives matter.

Do edibles work differently?
Yes—edibles/oral THC can have delayed onset and longer duration, increasing accidental overconsumption risk.

What’s an oromucosal spray?
A spray used in the mouth that can provide more consistent dosing than smoking and different onset vs swallowed products.

Do topicals (creams) work?
People use them for localized pain, but strong clinical evidence for many topical claims is limited.

What does “full-spectrum” mean?
It usually means multiple cannabinoids/compounds remain; labeling and quality vary by manufacturer and regulation.

What does “isolate” mean?
A single compound (e.g., CBD isolate); it can be more predictable but isn’t automatically safer or more effective.

What does “ratio” (e.g., 10:10 THC:CBD) mean?
It describes the relative amounts of THC and CBD, which can affect intoxication, side effects, and symptom response.

How do you choose THC vs CBD products?
Choice depends on symptom target, impairment tolerance, mental health risk, driving/work needs, and medication interactions—best decided with a clinician.

What’s “microdosing” cannabis?
Using very small THC doses to reduce side effects; evidence is still developing and dosing should be clinician-guided.

How do labels (mg/mL, mg/g) translate to dosing?
They indicate concentration; your actual dose depends on volume/amount used—misreading labels is a common cause of overuse.

Why do products feel different even with the same THC mg?
Route, absorption, CBD presence, tolerance, food intake, and individual metabolism all change effect.

Can you build tolerance?
Yes—repeated THC exposure can lead to tolerance, requiring higher doses for the same effect and increasing side-effect risk.

Safety, side effects, and risks (66–85)

What are the most common side effects?
Drowsiness, dizziness, dry mouth, mood/cognitive changes; THC can cause anxiety/paranoia in some people.

Can CBD cause side effects?
Yes—CBD can cause drowsiness, diarrhea/GI symptoms, appetite changes, fatigue, and can interact with medications.

Can CBD affect the liver?
CBD has been associated with liver injury in some contexts and can interact with other drugs—medical supervision matters.

Does THC affect memory and thinking?
THC can impair attention, memory, reaction time, and decision-making—especially at higher doses or in new users.

Can medical cannabis trigger psychosis?
High-THC use is associated with increased risk of psychotic outcomes in vulnerable individuals; risk varies with dose, frequency, age, and personal/family history.

Is cannabis addictive?
Cannabis use disorder can occur; risk increases with earlier age of use, high potency, and frequent use.

What are withdrawal symptoms?
Some people experience irritability, sleep problems, reduced appetite, and cravings after stopping frequent use.

Can cannabis cause vomiting in heavy users?
Yes—cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome can cause repeated severe nausea/vomiting in some heavy users.

Is it safe for older adults?
Older adults may be more sensitive to dizziness, falls, confusion, and drug interactions—clinician oversight is important.

Is it safe during pregnancy or breastfeeding?
Health authorities generally advise avoiding cannabis during pregnancy/breastfeeding due to potential risks to fetal/infant development.

Can teens use medical cannabis?
Youth are at higher risk of brain/mental health harms; paediatric use is typically restricted to specific indications under specialist care.

Does cannabis affect the heart?
Cannabis can affect heart rate and blood pressure; people with cardiovascular risk should be cautious and seek medical advice.

Can it affect lungs?
Smoking cannabis exposes lungs to irritants/toxins; non-smoked routes reduce combustion exposure.

Does cannabis interact with alcohol or sedatives?
Combining THC with alcohol or sedating meds can increase impairment and accident risk.

Can it worsen anxiety?
Yes—THC can worsen anxiety/panic in some people, especially at higher doses or in those prone to anxiety.

Can it worsen depression?
Some users report short-term relief, but heavy use may worsen mood outcomes for some; it’s not a proven antidepressant therapy.

Can it affect fertility?
Some reviews flag potential reproductive effects (e.g., noted concerns around CBD in certain contexts); evidence is still developing.

Can you safely stop suddenly?
Many people can stop, but frequent/heavy users may have withdrawal; discuss tapering and symptom management with a clinician if needed.

What’s the biggest “hidden” risk with medical cannabis?
Impairment + driving/work safety + drug interactions—these cause real harm even when the intent is medical.

Is online medical cannabis info reliable?
Not always—misinformation is common on social platforms, so prioritise government/health-organisation sources and clinician advice.

Driving, testing, and daily life (86–100)

Can you drive after taking THC medicinal cannabis? (Australia)
Guidance commonly says don’t drive with THC; impairment and crash risk increase and roadside tests can be positive.

How long after oral THC could driving risk last?
Oral THC effects (and impairment risk) can last for hours—healthdirect and RACGP note risk can last up to ~8 hours for oral THC products.

Does CBD affect driving?
There’s no strong evidence CBD impairs driving, but it can cause drowsiness/fatigue in some, which can still be unsafe.

Will you fail a drug test with THC?
THC can be detected after use; detection windows vary by dose, frequency, and test type.

Can “CBD-only” products trigger THC positives?
Some products may contain trace THC or be mislabeled (especially unregulated markets), so risk isn’t zero.

Can you take medicinal cannabis at work?
It depends on your job (especially safety-sensitive roles) and local policy; THC impairment is a major concern.

Can you take it with opioids?
Combination increases sedation/impairment risk; any combined regimen should be clinician-managed.

Can you take it with antidepressants or anxiety meds?
Potential interactions and additive sedation are possible; always check with a clinician/pharmacist.

Can you take it with blood thinners?
CBD can interact with some medications (Mayo Clinic specifically flags interactions such as with blood thinners), so medical guidance is important.

What should you do if you feel too high or panicky?
Stop dosing, move to a safe environment, avoid driving, and seek medical help if severe; edibles can last longer, so symptoms may persist.

How do you store medicinal cannabis safely?
Keep it sealed, out of reach of children/pets, and follow pharmacy label/storage instructions. (Accidental ingestion is a known hazard.)

Can pets take medical cannabis?
Do not give human cannabis products to pets unless a veterinarian specifically prescribes; THC can be toxic to animals.

Is “start low, go slow” good advice?
Yes—many clinical approaches emphasise cautious titration to reduce side effects, especially with THC and oral products.

Should you use dispensary advice as medical advice?
Be cautious—studies and reporting highlight that retail advice can exceed the evidence base; a clinician is best for medical decisions.

What’s the safest “rule of thumb” for medical cannabis?
Use regulated products when possible, involve a clinician, avoid driving/operating machinery with THC, and treat it like any other medicine with real risks and interactions
